When the Manufacturer Has to Step In, It's Already Too Late

On February 19, 2026, Altitude Rides and Attractions issued a safety-critical service bulletin for every Soaring Eagle Zipline Ride ever built. The problem? Operators weren't positioning restraints correctly. The fix required immediate retraining across every installation worldwide. This was preventable.

⚠ Safety-Critical SB-SEZ-02-2026  ·  Feb 19, 2026

Manufacturer Service Bulletin: Soaring Eagle Zipline Ride

Altitude Rides and Attractions, LLC has identified a safety-critical hazard involving improper positioning of belt restraints by ride operators. Incorrect restraint positioning has been determined to be a contributing factor in incidents and near-miss events, creating a serious risk of partial or full rider ejection.

The bulletin affects all models, all serial numbers, all production years. Two required actions: (1) Immediate operator retraining on updated restraint procedures. (2) Installation of updated shoulder restraint components within 120 days — ordered from and installed by Altitude Rides or qualified personnel.

What Happened

The bulletin is unambiguous: ride operators at Soaring Eagle Zipline installations have been improperly positioning lap belt restraints on riders. Two specific failure modes were identified.

First, riders being seated on top of the lap belts rather than under them — meaning the rider is effectively unrestrained in the passenger cart. Second, lap belts being positioned too high — across the abdomen or waist rather than low and tight across the hips and pelvic area, with specific attention required for riders with larger abdomens whose belt diameter may exceed hip diameter. In both cases, the manufacturer determined the risk outcome is the same: partial or full rider ejection.

The bulletin requires two distinct corrective actions. First, an immediate operator retraining on updated restraint positioning and verification procedures. Second, a physical hardware modification within 120 days: installation of a new updated shoulder restraint kit that relocates the unmonitored secondary belt from a lap-belt configuration to a shoulder-belt configuration, with new mounting brackets bolted to the cart structure. These components must be ordered directly from Altitude Rides and Attractions and installed by Altitude or qualified personnel. Only authorized components may be used.

This isn't just a procedural update. It's a combined operator performance failure and equipment redesign — an acknowledgment that the original restraint configuration, combined with operator behavior in the field, created a hazard that required both retraining and physical modification to resolve. And it was widespread enough that the manufacturer issued a bulletin covering every unit ever produced.

The Real Problem: Training Drift

Soaring Eagle Zipline Rides are marketed — correctly — as simple to operate. One operator. Seated cart. Electronic restraint monitoring. No harnesses. The simplicity is a selling point. But simplicity can breed complacency.

When a ride is "easy to operate," training programs tend to be brief. Refresher training tends to be rare. And the subtle but critical details — like exactly where a lap belt needs to sit on a rider's body, how that changes based on the rider's size and body type, and that operators must physically pull each belt snug before dispatch rather than relying on the HMI indicator lights — get lost over time.

Operators learned "how we do it here." They were never continuously verified against how the ride was designed to run.

This is what we call training drift — the gradual divergence between the manufacturer's operational intent and the park's day-to-day practice. It doesn't happen overnight. It happens one shortcut at a time, one new hire at a time, one season at a time. And by the time it surfaces as an incident or a near-miss, the manufacturer is forced into the most expensive and disruptive corrective action available: a universal service bulletin.

This Was Preventable

Every consequence described above — the emergency retraining, the equipment modifications, the insurance impact, the litigation exposure — traces back to a single root cause: no one was independently verifying that operators were running the ride to the manufacturer's spec.

The parks relied on their own internal training. The manufacturer relied on the parks to follow the manual. And no one was in the field, systematically observing and documenting whether lap belts were being positioned correctly on actual riders during actual operations.

The Alternative

What Independent Operator Verification Would Have Caught

If facilities operating Soaring Eagle Zipline Rides had an independent Operator Verification program in place — with auditors trained to the manufacturer's operational spec — here's what changes:

  • Restraint positioning is observed and documented on every audit visit — verifying riders are seated with hips against the seat back, lap restraints are low and tight across hips and pelvis (not waist or abdomen), and shoulder restraints are diagonal across the chest
  • Operator verification behavior is assessed: are operators physically pulling each belt snug before dispatch, or are they relying on HMI indicator lights alone? The bulletin is explicit that indicator lights are never sufficient
  • Rider body type variability — the exact issue highlighted in the bulletin — is monitored as a specific audit criterion, because riders with larger abdomens require particular attention to ensure the belt is positioned under the abdomen and tight against the hips
  • The "stop loading" protocol is verified: when an improper restraint is found, does the operator stop the loading process immediately, instruct riders to stand, and restart the procedure? Or do they adjust on the fly?
  • Remediation is tracked and verified. It's not enough to flag the issue — you need documented proof that the operator corrected the behavior and maintained it
  • The data is shared with the facility and, if the manufacturer opts in, with the OEM — creating an early warning system that identifies drift before it becomes a bulletin

The restraint positioning issue described in SB-SEZ-02-2026 doesn't develop overnight. It develops over weeks and months of unchecked operator behavior. An independent verification program catches it at week one — not after it becomes a contributing factor in incidents.

For Manufacturers: Protect Your Sales Spec and Your Reputation

This bulletin puts Altitude Rides and Attractions in a difficult position. They built a ride with electronic restraint monitoring, simplified operations, and clear documentation. And to their credit, the corrective action is thorough — not just a retraining memo, but a physical redesign that relocates the secondary belt from lap to shoulder configuration with new mounting hardware. They're doing the engineering right, again.

But the root cause remains: there was no mechanism to verify that operators in the field were following the original spec before the problem became systemic. That's the gap the Amusement Standards Manufacturer Partnership Program is designed to fill. When operators are verified to the manufacturer's standard — not just the park's interpretation of it — the ride performs the way it was designed to. Restraints are positioned correctly. Procedures are followed. And the manufacturer has documented, third-party evidence that their operational intent is being executed.

The alternative is what we're seeing now: a retroactive, industry-wide corrective action that costs everyone — manufacturer and operator alike — far more than a proactive verification program ever would.

For Operators: Don't Wait for the Bulletin

If you operate a Soaring Eagle Zipline Ride, you're now required to retrain immediately, verify interim operating conditions are being met daily, and install the shoulder restraint kit within 120 days. After the compliance period, operation without the modification is prohibited. That's the reactive path.

The proactive path is having a third-party verification program that continuously confirms your operators are running every ride — not just zip lines — to the manufacturer's spec. So the next time a bulletin is issued for any ride in your fleet, your response isn't scrambling. It's pulling up your audit records and showing that you were already in compliance.

That's a different conversation with your insurer. A different conversation with your regulator. And a different conversation with a plaintiff's attorney.
